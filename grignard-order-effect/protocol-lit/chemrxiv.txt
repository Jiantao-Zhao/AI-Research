Abstract: Organomagnesium halides (Grignard reagents) are essential carbanionic building
blocks widely used in carbon-carbon and carbon-heteroatom bond forming reactions with various
electrophiles. In the Barbier variant of the Grignard synthesis, the generation of air- and moisture-
sensitive Grignard reagents occurs concurrently with their reaction with an electrophile. Although
operationally simpler, the classic Barbier approach suffers from low yields due to multiple side
reactions, limiting its application scope. Here, we report a mechanochemical adaptation of the Mg-
mediated Barbier reaction, which overcomes these limitations and makes it suitable for the
coupling of versatile organic halides (allylic, vinylic, aromatic, aliphatic) with diverse range of
electrophilic substrates (aldehydes, ketones, esters, amides, O-benzoyl hydroxylamine,
chlorosilane, borate ester) to assemble C–C, C–N, C–Si, and C–B bonds. In contrast to the classic
two-step Grignard synthesis, the mechanochemical approach offers the advantages of being
essentially solvent-free, single step and operationally simple, immune to air, and surprisingly
tolerant to water and other proton donors. Mechanistic studies clarify the role of
mechanochemistry in the process, indicating that the reaction predominantly proceeds via
generation of transient organometallics, which occurs rapidly due to improved mass transfer and
activation of the surface of magnesium metal.

Introduction

In recent years, mechanochemistry has become increasingly popular as an essentially
solvent-free methodology for organic synthesis.1a-e Empowered by various instrumental
techniques,1f-h    mechanochemical          synthesis     allows     to    perform     miscellaneous
transformations without solvents or by using solvents in catalytic amounts (liquid assisted
grinding technique, LAG).1e,2 This offers several advantages over traditional solution-
based methods, such as fast reaction rates, reduced safety hazards and environmental
impact.3 In addition, mechanochemical activation significantly increases reactivity and
                                                   1
catalytic properties of solids, including metals.4 This advantage improves the performance
of metal-catalyzed transformations and allows preparation of organometallic reagents with
efficacy that is often unattainable in conventional solution-based chemistry.5




Scheme 1. The keynote previous works on mechanochemical Grignard synthesis and outline of this work.


Many classic organic reactions have been adapted to the mechanochemical approach,
including the venerable Grignard reaction. For the latter, avoiding or minimizing the use
of flammable and peroxide-forming ethereal solvents (Et2O, THF) allows to substantially
attenuate safety hazards.6 The mechanochemical Grignard reaction was pioneered by
Harrowfield and co-workers in 2001,7 who reported the first successful solvent-free
preparation of a naphthyl Grignard reagent followed by its reaction with ketones (Scheme
1, a). The reaction delivered a mixture of products besides the anticipated tertiary alcohol.
Despite a few sporadic examples reported afterwards,8,9 the synthetic utility of the
mechanochemistry-driven Grignard reaction has not been greatly expanded until very

                                                 2
recently. In 2021, the groups of Ito, Kubota10 and Bolm11 independently reported the facile
generation of versatile Grignard reagents using THF or 2-MeTHF as liquid additives by
ball milling, followed by their subsequent reactions with various electrophiles. However,
prior preparation of air- and moisture-sensitive Grignard reagents was necessary in these
works, what resulted in partial oxidation10 of organometallic intermediate or required inert
atmosphere protection.11
Here we report that many of synthetically useful transformations of organomagnesium
nucleophiles can be efficiently performed under mechanochemical conditions by
generating Grignard reagents in situ from magnesium and the respective organic halide
(i.e., in the Barbier variant). A variety of electrophilic reaction partners are compatible with
this single-step reaction design (Scheme 1, b). The proposed alternative is operationally
simpler, inherently immune to air, and remarkably tolerant to moisture and some other
proton sources. Furthermore, the mechanochemical approach retains the high efficiency
of the generic Grignard synthesis, delivering similar yields and exhibiting a broad scope
of amenable organic halides and electrophilic substrates. This outperforms the traditional
solution-based Mg-mediated Barbier reaction which delivers low and irregular yields12
except the reaction of allylic halides.13,14 The previously reported mechanochemical
Barbier reactions with zinc15 and bismuth16 demonstrate similar scope limitations when
compared with the newly developed method.

The three most important factors enabling high yields of alcohols 2a-d have been revealed
during the optimization studies for the synthesis of 2a (Table 2): (i) presence of THF (at
least 3 equiv.) which acts as a Lewis base ligand to stabilize organomagnesium
intermediate (Entry 1 vs 2 and 3), (ii) milling frequency, with yields of 2a remarkable
improved at high frequencies (Entry 1 vs 4 and 5, Table S2 in ESI), and (iii) surface area
of Mg metal, i.e., smaller particles (Entry 6 vs 8) or higher loading (Entry 6 vs 7) resulted
in better yields (Entries 6–8). The latter factor indicated the on-surface nature of the
reaction, while the dependence on milling frequency clearly manifested mechanochemical

origin of the observed yield enhancement. In line with these results, the best outcome
(98% yield of 2a, Entry 1) was obtained with Mg powder activated by a prior ball milling (3
h at 30 Hz). After the activation, the powder attained a distinct metal luster because of the
removed passivating “oxide” layer.17 The beneficial effect of the activated powder was
especially clear in the Barbier reactions of other halides, e.g., yield of 2b was noticeably
improved from 72% to 94% (Table S4). The mechanochemical synthesis of 2a was fast
and delivered 93% yield already after 10 min of milling at 30 Hz (Table S3). Importantly,
no induction delay was noted. The delay is common for the solution-based Grignard and
Barbier reactions and may lead to sudden exothermic initiation with risk of thermal
runaway.6 Inspection with a thermal camera (Fig. S4, ESI) revealed only insignificant
temperature increase outside and inside the milling jars (did not exceed 29°C). Despite
the exothermic nature of the reaction, the rather low temperature increase indicated
efficient heat dissipation and implied that thermal activation is an unlikely cause for the
short induction period and fast reaction rate.

Tolerance of a presumably organomagnesium-mediated processes to Brønsted acid
(NH4Cl) was extraordinary, since Grignard reagents are known as eager proton acceptors.
This intriguing outcome led us to investigate the limits of such tolerance in more detail in
the reactions of aldehyde 1, given high practical significance and mechanistic relevance
                                                     8
of these results. We found that the Barbier reactions of aldehyde 1 (Table 4, Scheme 3)
occurred not only with NH4Cl but also in the presence of water, although the yields of
alcohols 2a-d were 30–40% lower than those obtained in the additive-free processes.
Remarkably, water (1 equiv.) completely inhibited the same reactions in THF solution,
which did not start even after prolonged (24 h) stirring at room temperature or heating.
The failure could be attributed to passivation of magnesium surface with Mg(OH)2,14 while
the passive layer is readily removed in the ball milling process. The mechanochemistry-
initiated reactions of 1 with 2-phenylethyl bromide (Scheme 3) showed that yields of 2d
were reduced due to competitive protonation of organomagnesium intermediate 9-Mg, a
process that produced ethyl benzene 9. The generation of transient Grignard reagent
9-Mg and proton transfer from the ammonium salt have been confirmed in the experiment
with ND4Cl, which afforded the corresponding deuterium-labelled hydrocarbon 9-d in 40%
yield. In the reaction with water (1 equiv.), 9 was formed in 28% yield, indicating on
plausible intermediacy of 9-Mg and its fast addition to a carbonyl group of 1 concurrently
with hydrolysis.18 The similar behavior of liquid water and solid NH4Cl under
mechanochemical conditions was also notable (Table 4) and could be explained by
coordination of water to Mg2+ in a solid phase, a process which may also account for the
attenuated reactivity of water.18 The extent of protonation for 9-Mg did not correlate with
pKa values of the selected proton donors, indicating that Brønsted acidity is not a relevant
factor (Table S9). Besides ammonium chloride, K2HPO4 was identified as a promising Mg
alkoxide quencher, which afforded 2d in 81% yield (Scheme 3). In contrast to water, the
Barbier reaction did not occur in the presence of PhOH and i-PrOH.
After establishing the optimal conditions for the synthesis of alcohols from aldehydes and
ketones, a broader scope of organic halides and a more diverse set of electrophilic
reaction partners have been evaluated. Besides additional examples of functionalized
aldehydes and ketones, we tested the reactions of esters, amides, and several non-
carbonyl electrophiles that furnish C–Si, C–B and C–N bonds (Scheme 4).

In its reaction with aldehyde 1, cinnamyl chloride reacted almost exclusively with allylic
rearrangement, affording alcohol 2e in 70% yield of isolated product and as a 2:1 mixture
of anti- and syn-diastereomers (Scheme 4, a). Benzyl bromide smoothly produced the
corresponding alcohol 2f (69% yield). Among additional examples of aliphatic halides, n-
butyl bromide showed high efficacy and produced 2g in 83% yield, while methyl carbinol
                                                      10
2h was obtained in a modest 50% yield in the reaction with methyl iodide. Nevertheless,
the reaction with CH3I occurred even though the iodide passivates Mg in THF, rendering
it unsuitable for preparing the respective Grignard reagent.19 The reaction of 1 with
bromocyclohexane required an excess of activated Mg (12 equiv.) to attain 2i in 50% yield.
Notably, the reaction of 1 with c-C6H11MgBr in THF solution afforded the same outcome.
2-Bromopropene featured the corresponding addition product 2j in 73% yield, indicating
that vinylic bromides are also amenable substrates. Among the functionalized aromatic
bromides, p-OMe and p-Cl-substituted bromobenzenes have been successfully
employed, featuring alcohols 2k and 2l. In these two cases, additional activation of Mg
with a crystal of iodine was required to trigger the reactions. Synthesis of 2l also features
a chemoselective transformation in which C–Cl bond remained intact due to its much
lower reactivity in comparison with the arylic C–Br bond. Higher reactivity of bromides over
chlorides is common for the preparation of the respective Grignard reagents in solution,
for which the reaction rate decreases in the order C–I > C–Br > C–Cl > C–F.12b For the
mechanochemical Barbier reaction, we found that aliphatic and aromatic chlorides were
unreactive in comparison with the respective bromides (Scheme 4, b), while allylic and
benzylic chlorides demonstrated high reactivity comparable to their corresponding
bromides. The yield of 2c was lower for iodobenzene (55%) compared to bromobenzene
(92%) due to an intensified Wurtz coupling reaction that result in biphenyl formation (ca.
50% yield). Relative reactivity of organic bromides has been investigated in a series of
competition experiments (see section 2.7 in ESI) and revealed the following order:
CH2=CHCH2Br > EtBr > PhBr > PhCH2CH2Br. This order was also similar to that observed
in their reaction with Mg in Et2O solution, leading to the formation of corresponding
Grignard reagents.20 Notably, more reactive halides usually delivered better yields of
alcohols in their reactions with carbonyl substrates (Tables 1, 3) and showed better
tolerance to water and NH4Cl (Table 4). Because of the accompanying protonation of
organometallic intermediate (Scheme 3), the use of NH4Cl for in situ release of alcoholic
product from the respective Mg alkoxide is justified only when (i) organic halide delivers
highly reactive organometallic intermediate, (ii) to suppress undesired enolization, or (iii)
when a streamlined work-up protocol is advantageous because of technological reasons.

In a gram-scale preparation of alcohol 2d (Scheme 5), a comparison was made between
the developed one-step approach and the two-step mechanochemical Grignard synthesis.
The results revealed the superiority of the single-step Barbier protocol, which produced
2d in a noticeably better yield (75% vs 49%) from stoichiometric amounts of 1 and

                                                13
2-phenylethyl bromide. The two-step method was found to be susceptible to oxidation of
the Grignard intermediate 9-Mg in the presence of air, which resulted in the formation of
alcohol 33 (12% yield). This led to intensification of the MPV reaction as accompanying
side process (Table S11). In sharp contrast, the one-step protocol was found to be
immune to the oxidation process, yielding only a trace of 33. In addition, we successfully
performed a gram-scale allylation of 2-acetylnaphthalene 7 using NH4Cl as an additive.
Alcohol 6a was isolated in 96% yield after the treatment of the obtained paste-like reaction
mixture with ethyl acetate followed by filtration and evaporation of the solvent. Although
no significant safety hazards have been encountered during our work with Mg powder,
less reactive Mg beads looked preferable from the safety standpoint, especially in the
development of upscaled preparations. Keeping in mind that only high surface area of Mg
is crucial for attaining high yields, gram-scale synthesis of 6a was repeated with excess
(10 equiv.) of less reactive Mg beads and furnished 6a in 86% yield. The beads were not
fragmented during milling and eroded from the surface, what allowed easy recovery of the
excessive Mg (Fig. S9, ESI). Nevertheless, organic halides that may react violently or
even explosively with Mg (e.g., CF3-containing aromatic bromides)24 must be avoided in
mechanochemical preparations. For the substrates with unknown reactivity with Mg metal,
it is advisable to conduct safety investigations25 prior to small-scale test experiments.
The deuterium quenching experiment using ND4Cl (Scheme 3) provided conclusive
evidence for Grignard reagent as intermediate, which adds to carbonyl group of 1 to form
magnesium alkoxide product 2-Mg (Scheme 6, a). A competing MPV reaction of 2-Mg
with the remaining aldehyde can lead to the formation of side products 3 and 5, especially
if the consumption of 1 in its reaction with the Grignard reagent is relatively slow. Since
the addition of Grignard reagents to carbonyl compounds is typically very fast,21,26 we
concluded that Grignard reagent formation (GRF) is the rate-limiting step of the whole
process. To minimize the competitive reactions, GRF must be greatly intensified under
the mechanochemical conditions to ensure fast consumption of aldehyde. This conclusion
is supported by previous kinetic studies of GRF in solution, which have shown that the
rate of GRF depends on area and physical characteristics of the magnesium surface (also
clearly observed in our experiments), the viscosity of the medium, and the stirring
rate.20,27,28 The latter two factors are important because organic bromides often react with

Although the formation of organometallic intermediates was apparent, the possibility of
additional radical-mediated pathways could not be entirely disregarded.30,31 These
pathways have been suggested as alternative mechanism for the Barbier reaction,12c in
which radical and radical-anion species that mediate GRF17,32 may be intercepted by the
carbonyl substrate 1 or by the anion-radical 1·− generated from it (Scheme 6, a). The latter
can also produce pinacols 4, which were formed in significant amounts only in the
reactions of electron-deficient aldehydes. The organometallic-free mechanism could also
account for the formation of alcohol products in the presence of water.14,31 Therefore,
additional mechanistic experiments have been carried out with aim to detect whether the
contribution of the radical intermediates is significant, especially in the presence of proton
sources and in the reactions of electron-deficient carbonyl substrates. First,
cyclopropylmethyl bromide (35) was utilized as a radical clock probe (Scheme 6, c).33 The
reaction yielded a mixture of alcohols 2m and 2n in almost equal ratios of 1:1, regardless
of the presence or absence of proton sources (H2O or NH4Cl). The observation of an equal
ratio of 2m and 2n is consistent with the rapid formation17 of Grignard reagent C from the
cyclopropylmethyl radical A, which occurs with the same high rate as its intramolecular
rearrangement into B (k ~ 108 s−1).34 The short-lived alkyl radicals are unlikely to be
intercepted by 1 or its anion-radical 1·−, as these second-order processes either have
much lower rate constants (such as the radical addition to 1)35 or require high
concentrations of radical species (such as 1·−)36 to proceed at a competitive rate.
Moreover, if the organometallic-mediated pathway does not operate in the presence of
proton donors, the radical mechanism should predominantly deliver the rearranged
alcohol 2n. However, both NH4Cl and H2O reduced yields of the alcohols without altering
their ratio. This outcome is consistent with the competitive protonation and carbonyl
addition of the organometallic intermediates C and D.18 Based on these findings, we
anticipated that substrates that generate more stable and long-lived radical or radical
anion species, such as aromatic aldehydes (particularly those with electron-withdrawing
substituents) and allyl halides, may be more prone to react via the radical mechanism. To

test this hypothesis, we selected the allylation reactions of p-methoxy- and
p-chlorobenzaldehydes (Scheme 6, d) as an appropriate mechanistic probe due to the
distinct electronic properties of these substrates (Hammett constants of p-substituents, σp
= −0.27 and +0.23, respectively). It has been previously shown that the rate of anion-
radical-mediated Barbier reaction of benzaldehydes is highly sensitive to the nature of
p-substituents (kCl/kOMe = 37, based on Hammett equation).31 In contrast, the addition of
allyl Grignard reagents to carbonyl compounds is extremely fast and therefore non-
selective.21 In the competition experiments we performed, the Barbier reaction in aqueous
THF produced p-Cl-substituted alcohol 11 as the kinetic product, while the addition of
allylmagnesium chloride in THF solution was non-selective. The mechanochemical
Barbier reaction (with and without NH4Cl and H2O additives) delivered product ratios close
to 1:1.5 in slight favour of p-Cl-substituted product 11. Notable, p-chlorobenzaldehyde was
also involved into accompanying pinacol coupling. These results indicate on operation of
the anion-radical-mediated pathway, however as a minor contributor that becomes
apparent for electron-deficient aldehydes. A greater contribution of the radical mechanism
could be expected for carbonyl substrates which are more prone to single electron
reduction and produce long-living ketyl radicals, such as benzophenones. However, this
hypothesis has not been verified in our study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, here we demonstrate that Grignard synthesis can be expediently performed
in a single step under mechanochemical conditions via organomagnesium intermediates
generated in situ in the presence of several electrophilic reaction counterparts (Barbier
conditions). Essentially, high reactivity of organomagnesium compounds and reduced
side processes enabled by mechanochemistry allowed to expand the scope of Barbier
reaction beyond the conventional allylation of aldehydes. A broad range of usable
electrophiles   (aldehydes,   ketones,   esters,   amides,    chlorosilane,   borate   ester,
hydroxylamine) and organic halides (allylic, aromatic, aliphatic) are compatible with the
single-step reaction design, which is essentially solvent-free, features short reaction times
and operationally simple protocol. The yields of products obtained from 2-naphthaldehyde
were considerably better than in analogous solution based Barbier reaction and attained
high efficiency of the classic Grignard synthesis. By contrast to the latter, the developed

                                             17
one-step process is resistant to air oxidation, can be performed without protective inert
atmosphere and shows unusually high tolerance to water and some other proton donors.
The solid proton-donating salts (e.g., NH4Cl) can be used to release the alcoholic products
from the respective alkoxides in situ, streamlining the work-up protocol and suppressing
enolization side process. Mechanistic studies have indicated that the organomagnesium-
mediated pathway is the dominant mechanism at least in the reactions of aldehydes, with
ball milling process resulting in accelerated generation of transient Grignard reagents by
improving mass transfer and activating the surface of magnesium metal. The contribution
of alternative radical-mediated mechanistic scenarios has been evaluated and their minor
input in the reactions of aromatic aldehydes has been demonstrated. The developed
approach provides a convenient alternative to traditional Grignard synthesis, while the
established reactivity trends and mechanistic insights offer a starting point for further
innovations towards the greener and safer implementation of industrial organometallic
processes.
